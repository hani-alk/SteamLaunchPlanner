How it's calculated

Ranges, not predictions. Your inputs are the middle case. The low and high cases move the least certain inputs (conversion, buyers from outside your wishlists, the long tail, refunds) down and up around them. Real launches regularly land outside even this range.

Why the calendar window. Steam's Personal Calendar shows games with a public release date in the next eight weeks. Those late wishlists come from a calendar rather than from months of following the game, so they're counted separately and convert at their own rate.

  1. First-week copies (long-held × their rate + window × its rate) × beyond wishlists
  2. First-year copies first week × year-one sales
  3. Other points on the curve first week × weeks ^ (ln(year-one sales) ÷ ln 52) Copies pile up on a power curve anchored so week 52 lands on the first-year figure. The month reads off it at week 4. Years two and three extend the same curve and are the loosest part of the estimate.
  4. Copies you're paid for copies × (1 − refund rate)
  5. Average sale price price × (1 − erosion × (0.5 + 0.5 × share of the first year sold)) Week one feels half the erosion: regional pricing and VAT apply from day one, deeper discounts come later.
  6. Gross revenue copies paid for × average sale price
  7. Steam's cut 30% to $10M · 25% to $50M · 20% above
  8. Take-home (gross − Steam's cut) × (1 − withholding × US share of sales) Valve withholds US tax only on US-source income, meaning sales to customers in the US.

Assumptions

Window wishlists arrive evenly
The chart draws them as a straight ramp. In practice the day the date goes public is a spike; only the total matters to the estimate.
Gross is already net of VAT
Valve strips VAT before splitting revenue. That, plus regional pricing, is why erosion defaults well above zero.
Refunds are removed as copies
A refund costs the whole sale. Steam's share is charged on what's left.
This is revenue, not profit
Steam Direct, ports, marketing and your own costs aren't included.
Withholding follows your W-8BEN
Treaty rates vary; the list is a shortcut, not tax advice. It applies only to your US sales. US developers file a W-9 and see none.
Payment lags a month
Valve pays each month's earnings around the 30th of the next month, once you're over $100.

    How the forecast works

    Decay toward a tail. After the peak day, daily sales are modelled as falling by a fixed share each day toward a long-tail level, which itself drifts down slowly (about 50% a year). The daily fall is fitted from your last few days of decline and blended with a typical launch curve until there's enough data.

    The range. The low case falls faster and settles lower; the high case falls slower and settles higher. With a day or two logged the range is wide. It narrows over the first eight weeks as the curve firms up, but never closes.

    Money. Steam net per copy comes from your Steamworks figures when you enter them, otherwise from price, launch and planned discounts, VAT and regional pricing, and refunds. Payout applies Steam's tiered cut (30% to $10M, 25% to $50M, 20% above), then your withholding rate on the US share of sales.

    Discounts. Each planned discount multiplies the middle, low and high cases by its boost for its length. Boost is a guess; adjust it once you've run a sale.
